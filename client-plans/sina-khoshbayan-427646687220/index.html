Section 03

The biggest opportunities I see

If you only focus on a few things over the next 90 days, focus on these. In this order.

1. Decide who this is for

This is the first thing we address, on our first call, and it's the foundation everything else stands on. Not just psychographically, the values this person holds, but concretely: what type of project are they building? What's their role, a homeowner, a developer, a builder, a business owner opening a space? What's their budget? Where are they in the process when they find you? Right now we don't have a clear answer to any of that.

When you booked our first call, you wrote that your number one challenge was "finding and closing the RIGHT clients." I agree. And "right" has to be defined before it can be found. Our entire marketing strategy begins with answering this question. The specialists are the ones who get paid more, and you can't specialize in ten things.

2. Turn your philosophy into a position

Simple forms. Clean lines. Very little decoration. Materials and construction methods that shape the design instead of hiding behind it, set against luxury, bloated design that masks what things are made of and costs more than it should. Those are genuinely unique ideas, and they're a decent foundation to build on.

What's missing is the clear articulation: how, specifically, is the work you do different from what other studios offer, and why should someone hire you over them? I think we're part way there. The rest gets built after the who, because the articulation changes depending on who's listening. This is what lets you charge for what you do. The difference between a firm at $10 a square foot and a firm at $40 isn't the quality of the work. It's how it's positioned.

3. Put your face on the brand

Right now the personal brand of Sina and the business of Modernacy live on separate platforms. I think that's a mistake, and it's one of the strongest moves available to you right now. One of the most powerful differentiators you have in business is the ability to put a face to a brand. Merge the two, so that Modernacy's platform becomes a way of showing not just the work you do, but the people behind it and the perspective that shapes every decision in your design process.

This is an extension of your unique value proposition, another layer on top of it. People don't do business with faceless brands. They do business with people. The more you let your humanity lead in the marketing, the more room you give someone to feel a deeper resonance with what you do before they ever see a proposal. I want to work with you on anchoring this into a social and content strategy that lets you stand out and land on a deeper level with the people you're here to serve.

4. Build a real marketing engine, and use referrals on purpose in the meantime

There isn't a marketing process today, and I'm not going to pretend we can design one yet. The right activities vary widely depending on who the niche actually is. So the sequence is: once the who is clear, we anchor it into a specific set of marketing activities you can use to get in front of those people, build relationships with them, and create sales conversations where you explore how you can help on a deeper level. That's the longer-term play, and it's the engine that produces the clients you actually want.

In the meantime, if short-term revenue is a priority, I think there's untapped potential in your existing network. Referrals have been your best source of aligned clients. We can leverage them more fully and more deliberately to create opportunities and build runway while the engine gets built. The difference is between waiting for "do you know a good architect?" and asking specific people for specific introductions once you know exactly who you're looking for.

5. Get out of the weeds

You're the bottleneck because everything runs through you. This is the classic technician-turned-owner problem, and the way out isn't working harder. It's two things in order. First, narrow the lane. You can't build systems that let less skilled people do high-quality work when you're serving too many different groups; it becomes impossible to specialize your approach. Second, once the marketing strategy is in place, we extricate you from the doing and move you into an owner's mindset, where your focus is building process and systems so you can get out of the weeds and onto the big picture.

You're already on track with this. Mayan Pools runs without you. The constraint is that the rest of the offering is too broad to operationalize the same way. Sort that out, and we come back and replicate what you did with the pool work across everything else.

$50K to $80K a month isn't a bigger version of today's business. It's a narrower one. A specialist, with a face, that the right people seek out and pay accordingly.

Section 04

What needs to shift first

Before any tactic in this plan works, these have to move. They're not to-dos. They're the ground the to-dos stand on.

From craftsman to owner

This is the big one. Your biggest opportunity, at the highest level, is developing and cultivating the business side of your skill set to complement the architectural and design skills you already have. They are two different skills, and the tension between them is what you're feeling right now.

There's a book I want you to read in the first thirty days: The E-Myth, by Michael Gerber. It's about exactly this transition, the mindset of business ownership versus craftsmanship, and why so many technicians get stuck between the two. Its core idea is the one I'd tattoo on this plan: your job is to build systems that allow people less skilled than you to do high-quality work, rather than making yourself indispensable.

From "what makes us unique" to "who is it for"

On our first call I described the two sides of messaging as the artist and the copywriter. The artist is your design philosophy, and yours is strong. The copywriter asks a colder question: what problem are we solving, for which group of people, who will actually pay for it? You said it yourself on that call: "I need to go back from the artist towards the copywriter more to find a better balance." That's the shift. The artist doesn't go anywhere. The copywriter finally gets a seat.

From catch-all to specialist

Right now you're serving several different groups of people, selling design work into completely different niches. Until this business stops being a catch-all that serves everyone who comes in the door, it will never be known for, and stand for, something powerfully enough to command the premium rates you want to charge. From doing to leading

Positioning & messaging

That's raw material, not final copy. The exact words get sharpened after the who. But I want you to notice how different it feels from "modern, soulful design."

A position stated by a logo is a claim. The same position stated by the person who holds it, on camera, week after week, is proof.

Not another farmhouse replica. Buildings that are honest about what they're made of, simple in form, designed by people who'd rather save you money than spend it, and built by the same team that drew them. If that's what you want, you've found your people. If it isn't, you haven't. (A starting frame to refine together, not final copy.)

Section 08

Systems & operations

You're the bottleneck. The way out is narrower work, documented, and handed off. You've already done it once.

Why this comes after narrowing

Your work as the owner is to build systems that allow people who are less skilled than you to do high-quality work. The only way to get there is to narrow the service offering and the niche enough that your approach can become specialized. If you're serving too many different groups of people, it's impossible to systematize, because every project is a different kind of project. So step one is narrowing the lane and choosing the who. Step two, once the marketing strategy is in place, is extricating you from the doing. That's the order, and it's why I'm not asking you to overhaul operations in the first month.

Replicate what you did with Mayan Pools

You already have the proof that this works. The pool work has an intake form, a shared communication platform, and a team that delivers it with minimal involvement from you. You named the opportunity yourself: take what's working there and apply it to the rest of the firm. Once the offering is narrowed, we come back to this and do exactly that, line by line. The constraint was never your ability to build the system. It was that the rest of the business was too broad to build one for.

Put a dollar value on every task

Here's the exercise I want you to do in the first thirty days. Take the list of how you spend your week and put a dollar value on every task on it. Then figure out what an hour of your time is worth at your income goal. Call it $50K a month. Do that math for yourself, but roughly, an hour of the owner of that business is worth somewhere around $250 to $300.

Now sort the list. Some of what you're doing is $20 or $30 an hour work. Some of it is $1,000-plus an hour work: sales, calls with leads, proposals. Design work is something that should be outsourced to your team. Basic coordination and admin, the same. The way we grow this business, and the way we grow you as a leader, is when you get ruthless about handing off anything on your plate that's worth less per hour than the number you just calculated. That frees you up for the work that actually generates revenue.

Look at your own list through that lens. Fifteen hours of design, eight reviewing redlines, six coordinating, six on email, four on admin. That's roughly thirty-nine hours a week below the line, and it doesn't include the time-tracking spreadsheets you cross-reference by hand. Right now you're far too in the weeds. I want to spend time with you mapping out what this transition looks like in practice, but the sorting starts now.

Roles and ownership with Amir

This is the foundational piece. You see him running the design side while you lead the build side down the line, and you don't yet know whether he wants that. The business can't be systematized around two people whose roles are undefined. Have the conversation early, bring where you land to a call, and we'll build the operating structure on top of it. You already know this: you wrote that operations need "clearly defined roles and responsibilities, starting with my partner and me."

On letting go

You wrote that it's hard to let go of tasks, partly trust and partly "I haven't found the right person to train for those roles." I'd gently reframe that. It's hard to hand off work that lives in your head. It's easy to hand off work that lives in a process. That's the whole point of the E-Myth, and it's why documenting how you work comes before hiring for it.

Anything on your plate worth less per hour than the owner of a $50K-a-month firm gets handed off. That's not a luxury you earn later. It's the job now.

Days 31–60 Implementation — articulate the difference, fix the conversation, use the network
  1. Write the positioning. With the who settled: what's different about the work, how specifically it's different from the other studios in Atlanta, and why someone should hire Modernacy over them.   1. Launch the marketing activities we defined. The specific set from our calls, run on a consistent cadence, with two numbers tracked: leads per month and where each one came from. This replaces marketing by accident.
