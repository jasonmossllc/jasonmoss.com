Section 02

Where you are right now

You built a profitable, well-run practice by taking the work that came through the door. That's exactly what got you here. It's also exactly what's capping you now.

Let me start with what's true and good, because there's a lot of it and I don't want it to get buried under the rest.

What's working

You built something real, and you built it lean. You started the practice in 2020 because the job market had dried up, and it was profitable early. Six years later there are seven or eight of you, averaging about $20K a month with about $15K of it profit. Expenses around $5K. No business debt. Car and land paid off, $30K to $50K set aside for the cabin. That's the thing that gives you room to make the moves in this plan without panic. Most people in your position don't have that room.

You have a design philosophy with teeth. "We're not chasing 'luxury.' We're after functionality and efficiency." "A house built today can't, and shouldn't, fake that." "Architects shouldn't add cost to a project. We should save it." Most firms your size have a style. You have a point of view, and you can say it in plain English. That's rarer than you think, and it's the raw material for everything in Section 05.

You close. Seventy to eighty percent of the people you meet in person become clients. Once you're in the room, the sales process works, and your referral clients have been your most aligned clients.

You've already proven you can operationalize. The Mayan Pools work is 60% of your revenue and, in your words, it "now runs well with minimal involvement from me." Amir leads it, the drafting team delivers it. That's the template for the rest of the firm.

The reels on your personal account are good. I like them. "Once you learn to see architecture, you can't unsee it." The window grids. Compression and expansion. That's a person with a real perspective, talking to camera. Hold on to that. We're going to put it to work.

You already know what the next chapter is. You wrote "it all runs through me." You wrote "I'm moving from someone who does everything to someone who leads." That self-awareness matters more than any tactic in here.

What's holding you back

The short version: you're a craftsman running a business, and those are two completely different skills. I don't mean that in a negative way. It's where almost every entrepreneur starts, and it generally gets you to somewhere around six figures. Then it starts to break down. Here's what the breakdown looks like today:

  • Nobody can say who Modernacy is for. When I asked you to define your niche, you described what makes your work unique. That's positioning, and it's valuable. But it isn't a who. Your About page says you focus on "homes, boutique commercial spaces, and the occasional passion project that just feels right." Your Work page spans Atlanta, Costa Rica, Alaska, Ghana and Tanzania. Meanwhile the work that actually pays the team, pool drawings, custom homes for builders and developers, restaurants and medical offices, barely appears anywhere. There's even a tab of logo design. What a stranger sees is a studio that will take just about any project.
  • There's no marketing process. Your words: "Honestly, we don't have a real marketing process yet." Almost every lead is a referral. The Modernacy Instagram has 22 posts, 211 followers and hasn't posted since November 2024. The YouTube channel has four rendering walkthroughs from 2023. The personal reels are the one living thing, and you told me they "aren't aimed at a specific client."
  • The website stands at a distance. The homepage is an animated logo and two words: Work and About. The Work page shows ten small black-and-white thumbnails, so the most powerful thing you have, the spaces themselves, is the thing the site shows smallest. The About page lists four people, and Amir isn't one of them. I don't feel the humans behind the work when I visit.
  • Everything runs through you. Fifty-six to fifty-nine hours a week. Fifteen on design, eight reviewing the team's work, eight to ten on site, six coordinating, six on email, four on admin, and time tracking in spreadsheets you cross-reference by hand. "Apart from production and drafting, I handle almost everything myself."
  • Price is where you're losing. Since you raised rates toward $10 to $12 a square foot, you've been "losing noticeably more leads than before," compared against stock plans and low-cost architects. And you wrote "my confidence in pricing can improve."
  • The partner question is open. You see Amir running the design side while you lead the build side, and you wrote "I'm not sure yet whether he wants to join the construction journey the same way." That's a foundational question, not a someday question.

None of this is a talent problem or a quality problem. It's a positioning problem, a marketing problem, and an operating problem. All three are fixable, in that order. That order is this plan.

Until someone can say in one sentence who Modernacy is for, there is no marketing strategy to build, no premium to command, and no business to hand off. That one question is where we start.

Section 03

The biggest opportunities I see

If you only focus on a few things over the next 90 days, focus on these. In this order.

1. Decide who this is for

This is the first thing we address, on our first call, and everything else stands on it. Not just psychographically, the values this person holds, but concretely: what type of project are they building? What's their role, a homeowner, a developer, a builder, a business owner opening a space? What's their budget? Where are they in the process when they find you? When you booked our first call you wrote that your number one challenge was "finding and closing the RIGHT clients." I agree. And "right" has to be defined before it can be found. The specialists are the ones who get paid more, and you can't specialize in ten things.

2. Turn your philosophy into a position

Simple forms. Honest materials. No fake history. The architect who removes cost instead of adding it. Those are genuinely unique ideas and a decent foundation. What's missing is the articulation: how, specifically, is your work different from what other studios offer, and why should someone hire you over them? We build that after the who, because the answer changes depending on who's listening. This is what lets you charge for what you do. The difference between a firm at $10 a square foot and a firm at $40 isn't the quality of the work. It's how it's positioned.

3. Put your face on the brand

Right now Sina and Modernacy live on separate platforms. I think that's a mistake, and fixing it is one of the strongest moves available to you. Merge the two, so Modernacy's platform shows not just the work, but the people behind it and the perspective that shapes every decision in your design process. People don't do business with faceless brands. They do business with people. The more you let your humanity lead, the more room you give someone to feel a deeper resonance with what you do before they ever see a proposal.

4. Build one marketing engine, and use referrals on purpose in the meantime

There's no marketing process today, and I'm not going to pretend we can design one yet, because the right activities depend entirely on who the who turns out to be. Once that's clear, we anchor it into a specific set of activities to get in front of those people, build relationships with them, and create sales conversations. That's the longer-term play. In the meantime, if short-term revenue is a priority, there's untapped potential in your existing network. The difference is between waiting for "do you know a good architect?" and asking specific people for specific introductions once you know exactly who you're looking for.

5. Get out of the weeds

You're the bottleneck because everything runs through you, and the way out isn't working harder. It's narrowing the lane first, so your approach can become specialized enough to systematize, and then extricating you from the doing once the marketing strategy is in place. You're already on track with this. Mayan Pools runs without you. The constraint is that the rest of the offering is too broad to operationalize the same way. Sort that out, and we replicate what you did with the pool work across everything else.

$50K to $80K a month isn't a bigger version of today's business. It's a narrower one. A specialist, with a face, that the right people seek out and pay accordingly.

Section 04

What needs to shift first

Before any tactic in this plan works, these have to move. They're not to-dos. They're the ground the to-dos stand on.

From craftsman to owner

This is the big one. Your biggest opportunity, at the highest level, is developing the business side of your skill set to complement the architectural and design skills you already have. There's a book I want you to read in the first thirty days: The E-Myth, by Michael Gerber. It's about exactly this transition, and its core idea is the one I'd tattoo on this plan: your job is to build systems that allow people less skilled than you to do high-quality work, rather than making yourself indispensable.

From "what makes us unique" to "who is it for"

On our first call I described the two sides of messaging as the artist and the copywriter. The artist is your design philosophy, and yours is strong. The copywriter asks a colder question: what problem are we solving, for which group of people, who will actually pay for it? You said it yourself: "I need to go back from the artist towards the copywriter more to find a better balance." The artist doesn't go anywhere. The copywriter finally gets a seat.

From catch-all to specialist

Until this business stops being a catch-all that serves everyone who comes in the door, it will never be known for, and stand for, something powerfully enough to command the premium rates you want. Narrowing the offering considerably is going to feel like risk. We'll plan the transition so it doesn't put the business at risk. But the direction isn't negotiable if you want $10 to $12 a foot to be the floor and not the ceiling.

From doing to leading

You wrote it better than I could: "A lot of what holds me back, like hesitating on pricing, holding on to tasks, and letting uncertainty take up space in my mind, comes from still operating as the earlier version of myself." Hold that. Every time you're about to redline a drawing yourself, chase an invoice yourself, or coordinate a consultant call yourself, ask which version of you is doing it.

Two conversations to have early

Pricing confidence: "I want to feel more confident in our work and our pricing." I want to understand what actually stops you from feeling that your higher rates are more than worth it. There's mindset work for us to do there. And the roles between you and Amir: who owns what, what he wants from the design-build move. My sense is this is foundational and needs to be addressed early, because it's what we build everything else on.

You wrote: "I can probably benefit from less planning and overthinking and more action, moving forward before I have everything figured out." Yes. This plan is sequenced so you can act on it before every question is answered.

Section 05

Positioning & messaging

You have an artist's position. The copywriter hasn't shown up yet. The next 90 days are about getting the two into the same room.

What your copy says right now

Here's what a stranger reads today. The site's description tag: "Architecture and graphic design company based in Atlanta, GA." The About page: a studio that focuses on "homes, boutique commercial spaces, and the occasional passion project that just feels right," with "modern, soulful design – clean lines married to warm textures." The Instagram bio: "Efficiency, Accuracy, Delicacy, Modernacy." Those sentences could sit on a thousand studio websites. They describe a style. They don't say who it's for or why anyone should pay more for it.

Compare them with what you wrote in your assessment: "Much of American residential design today imitates homes built 100 years ago and expresses a history that isn't there." That sentence makes someone either lean in or leave. That's what positioning does. And one small thing that tells the whole story: the last posts on the Modernacy feed carry #luxuryhomes in the hashtags. You told me "we're not chasing luxury." Your feed says you are, because nobody has decided yet what Modernacy stands for, so the copy defaults to what everyone else says.

The order: who, then what's different, then why you

Positioning is built in a sequence, and skipping the first step is why the rest has stayed vague. First the who. Then what makes the work different, specifically, from the other studios in Atlanta. Then why someone should hire you over them. We do the first on calls one and two and work on the rest together once it's settled. A developer activating a lot and a couple building on land they've owned for years do not need the same sentence.

The raw material we already have

On our first call, when you described what you're not, I said it sounded like you're the anti-farmhouse guys. If someone wants another replica of a house pretending to be a hundred years old, you're not their people. If they want a building that's honest about what it's made of, simple in form, and designed by someone who'd rather remove cost than add it, you are. Add the line from your assessment, "designed and built by the same team," and you have the bones of a position nobody else in your market is standing on. That's raw material, not final copy. But notice how different it feels from "modern, soulful design."

This is also the answer to price. If you raise your rates but don't raise your positioning to match, people see you in the same bar you were in at $5 a foot, now asking for twice as much, and they walk. That's what's been happening since you raised rates, and positioning is the fix.

Not another farmhouse replica. Buildings that are honest about what they're made of, simple in form, designed by people who'd rather save you money than spend it, and built by the same team that drew them. If that's what you want, you've found your people. If it isn't, you haven't. (A starting frame to refine together, not final copy.)

Section 06

Offers & pricing

Three lines of business, three different buyers. A boutique firm has one.

What you sell today, and how we narrow it

Pool construction drawings for Mayan Pools, about 60% of revenue at $70 an hour, run by Amir and the drafting team. Custom residential, about 20%, priced per square foot, historically $5 to $6 and recently raised toward $10 to $12. Commercial and office, about 20%, same structure. And a Visual tab of logo design on the website. That's three distinct groups of people buying design work for completely different reasons, plus a fourth thing.

Delivery is working and I'm not here to fix what isn't broken. But the shape of the offer suite has to change: from a catch-all design firm to a highly specialized, narrowly defined boutique firm that delivers for a very specific group of people, with prices that match the caliber of specialty work you do. I don't want to guess at what we narrow to. What I need from you before our first call is a breakdown of the past year's clients by line of business: what percentage of revenue is pool work, residential, commercial, and anything else. It may be that we trim to what's bringing in 80% of the revenue, so the transition is a simplification rather than a leap. The breakdown is what lets us plan it without putting the business at risk.

Where design-build and the cabins fit

Your highest vision is clear and I'm holding it as a key point for us: design-build, starting with cabins, with the first one already underway. "I get much more satisfaction from solving them on the job site and watching things come to life than from solving them on paper." I take that seriously. Where it sits in the narrowed offering, and on what timeline, we decide together after the who and the breakdown. What I can tell you now is that "designed and built by the same team" is one of the most differentiated things you've said, and the narrowing should move you toward it.

Pricing

I agree with raising your rates and becoming more selective, and I don't want you to retreat on price because leads got harder. The path to being paid a premium is to narrow, define the who, and articulate a value proposition that makes Modernacy a specialized provider for a narrower slice of clients. Not by explaining the price better. By being the kind of firm the price makes sense for.

The sales conversation

Given your close rate, I'm not inclined to make major changes to the sales process right now. One caveat: that number was built on referral leads, which arrive warm. We'll watch whether it holds with non-referral leads, which are harder to close.

That said, losing noticeably more leads since raising prices tells me one of two things is happening. Either people aren't seeing you as differentiated enough, so they treat you as a commodity, which is a marketing problem and the main focus of this plan. Or the sales process isn't developing the value in a way that justifies the higher rate. I'm not convinced it's only the first one. So I want to reserve one of our sessions for sales coaching, where I look at how you actually run a lead from first text to signed proposal and we find the gaps. One thing I'll offer now: the key isn't communicating value better. It's asking the right questions, so the person across from you names the problem, what they want, and the distance between the two. Then you reflect what you do back as the bridge across that gap. Great sales conversations at higher prices aren't about talking better. They're about asking questions that help people sell themselves.

The key to higher pricing isn't a better explanation of your value. It's better questions, so the person across the table names the gap themselves and sees your work as the bridge.

Section 07

Marketing & content strategy

There is no marketing process today. We don't build one until the who is clear. Then we build exactly one, and we put your face on it.

Why I'm not naming channels yet

What I see from the marketing side is simple. One: get clear on who your ideal clients are. Two: get clear on what makes your work different and what your value proposition actually is. Three: anchor that into a marketing engine to get in front of those people at scale, along with a refreshed website and a social strategy that carries that clarity. I'm deliberately not telling you which activities to run yet, because the way you reach developers is not the way you reach a couple with land in the mountains. Once the who is answered, we define a specific set of activities and that becomes your marketing process. Intentional and consistent, like you asked for.

Merge the two accounts into one brand

Modernacy's platform stops being a quiet portfolio of renderings and becomes the place where the work, the people, and the perspective live together. The reels you're already making are the start. What changes is that they get pointed at the who, they live where the business lives, and they show the people behind the buildings. You said your team could help with content if there were a direction. Once the who and the position are set, there is one, and I'll work with you on anchoring it into a content strategy that lets you stand out and resonate on a deeper level with the people you're here to serve.

The website, after the clarity

The site gets the same treatment, and it comes second, because rewriting it before the who and the position would just be prettier vagueness. When we get there: the Vision copy rewritten for who the firm is for and what makes it different. The images of the spaces front and center, large and in color. More of you and the people behind the work. And the kind of work you want more of shown clearly, which the site isn't doing today.

Referrals, on purpose, for runway

While the engine gets built, use what already works. Once the who is defined, go back through past clients, the builders and developers who've sent you work, and the collaborators listed on your own About page, and ask specific people for specific introductions to the kind of client you've now defined. Short-term cash flow and runway, not the long-term strategy.

Section 08

Systems & operations

You're the bottleneck. The way out is narrower work, documented, and handed off. You've already done it once.

Narrow first, then replicate Mayan Pools

Your work as the owner is to build systems that let people less skilled than you do high-quality work, and that's only possible once the offering is narrow enough to specialize your approach. So step one is choosing the who. Step two, once the marketing strategy is in place, is extricating you from the doing. You already have proof this works: the pool work has an intake form, a shared communication platform, and a team that delivers it with minimal involvement from you. Once the offering is narrowed, we apply exactly that to the next line of business, and the next.

Put a dollar value on every task

Here's the exercise I want you to do in the first thirty days. Take the list of how you spend your week and put a dollar value on every task. Then figure out what an hour of your time is worth at your income goal. Call it $50K a month: roughly, an hour of the owner of that business is worth somewhere around $250 to $300. Now sort the list. Some of what you're doing is $20 or $30 an hour work. Some of it is $1,000-plus an hour work: sales, calls with leads, proposals. Design work should be outsourced to your team. Basic coordination and admin, the same. The way we grow this business, and the way we grow you as a leader, is when you get ruthless about handing off anything on your plate worth less per hour than the number you just calculated.

Through that lens, fifteen hours of design, eight reviewing redlines, six coordinating, six on email and four on admin is roughly thirty-nine hours a week below the line. Right now you're far too in the weeds. I want to map the transition with you, but the sorting starts now.

Roles with Amir, and letting go

The business can't be systematized around two people whose roles are undefined. Have the conversation early, bring where you land to a call, and we'll build the operating structure on top of it. And on letting go: you wrote it's partly trust and partly "I haven't found the right person to train for those roles." I'd gently reframe that. It's hard to hand off work that lives in your head. It's easy to hand off work that lives in a process. That's why documenting how you work comes before hiring for it.

Anything on your plate worth less per hour than the owner of a $50K-a-month firm gets handed off. That's not a luxury you earn later. It's the job now.

Section 09

Your 90-day action plan

This is the payoff. Concrete enough to start Monday. Sequenced on purpose, so don't reorder it.

Days 1–30 Foundation — define the who, get the facts on the table
  1. Bring the revenue breakdown to our first call. The past twelve months by line of business, pool drafting, residential, commercial and anything else, as a percentage of revenue. Hours too if you can get them.
  2. Price every task on your list. Dollar value per task, your hourly worth at $50K a month, everything sorted above or below that line. Bring the sorted list.
  3. Define the who, with me, on calls one and two. Project type, role, budget, where they are in the process when they find you. One paragraph, written down, that Amir and the team can repeat. Nothing else moves until this does.
  4. Have the roles conversation with Amir. Design side and build side, what he wants from the design-build move, who owns what. Bring where you land to a call.
  5. Read The E-Myth. Owner versus craftsman. It's the frame for days 31 through 90.
Days 31–60 Implementation — articulate the difference, fix the conversation, use the network
  1. Write the positioning. With the who settled: what's different, how specifically, and why Modernacy over the other studios in Atlanta. We refine the anti-farmhouse frame into real language. This becomes the spine of the site, the feed, and every proposal.
  2. Do the sales session with me. Bring two or three recent proposals that died on price. We rebuild the conversation around questions that help the client name the gap, and look at how you qualify before a site visit.
  3. Make deliberate referral asks. Past clients, the builders and developers who've sent you work, the collaborators on your About page. Specific people, specific introductions, aimed at the who.
  4. Decide the transition. From the breakdown and the who: what you keep, what you phase out, how, and over what period. Mayan Pools stays the stable base while you make the shift.
  5. Start the merge. Modernacy's platform begins showing the people and the perspective, not just renderings. Point your reels at the who and put your team on the editing.
Days 61–90 Momentum — the engine, the website, and your way out of the weeds
  1. Launch the marketing activities we defined. Run them on a consistent cadence with two numbers tracked: leads per month and where each one came from.
  2. Refresh the website. Copy for the who and the position, images large and in color, the people visible, the work you're phasing out gone.
  3. Document and hand off. Write down how the Mayan Pools work runs and apply it to the next line. Admin, invoicing and coordination off your plate. Design and redlines moving to the team. Your design hours visibly lower by day 90.
  4. Watch the numbers, and check in on pricing. Close rate on non-referral leads versus referral leads. Leads per month. How often price is why a lead goes quiet. Your hours above and below the line. If the close rate slips as leads get colder, we go back into the sales process. By now you've run the new positioning in real conversations, so we also look at what still stops you on price and what the next rate decision is.
Before you go

One last thing

This is a roadmap, not a rulebook. The sequence matters: the who, then what's different, then the engine, then the way out of the weeds. How it feels in practice is yours to shape. If something isn't landing, that's information. Bring it to me.

The core of it one more time. You built a profitable firm by taking the work that came through the door, and that was the right way to start. The next chapter is a narrower firm that stands for something specific, with your face and your perspective on it, run by a team that doesn't need you in every drawing, and priced like the specialty work it is. That's how you get to $50K to $80K a month and to the design-build life you actually want. Not by doing more. By doing less, more precisely.

Some of this will take longer than 90 days. Narrowing a business is a transition, and I'll help you plan it so the lights stay on. But the first moves, the who, the breakdown, the roles conversation, the dollar-value exercise, the book, are things you can start this week. When you hit something, a proposal that died on price, a conversation with Amir that got stuck, a task you can't make yourself hand off, that's exactly when to reach out. Not after you've figured it out alone.
